# Selected lessons from Masahiro Sakurai on Creating Games

These are concise paraphrases, not quotations or a transcript archive. Timestamp ranges refer to the linked videos. Source evidence was retrieved with `youtube-transcript-api` during the Ryuchii World battle-planning session; only Japanese automatic captions were available and they contain transcription imperfections. The English wording below is an interpretive summary of those caption notes, not an official translation. Confirm exact wording against the video before quoting it.

The application column contains our design interpretation. It is not a claim that Sakurai prescribed a particular mechanic, combat value, engine architecture, or workflow for every game.

| Lesson and source segment | Source paraphrase | Possible application |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Spec Changes — 00:15–00:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bzWibA7izF8&t=15s), [01:28–01:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bzWibA7izF8&t=88s), [02:20–03:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bzWibA7izF8&t=140s) | Building reveals unknowns, while revisions create rework across design, models, motion, effects, programming, and sound. Surface a known problem early and weigh immediate and later consequences. | Keep a brief change record with affected systems. Do not preserve a poor prototype decision simply because work already exists. |
| [Making Your Game Easy to Tune — 00:32–01:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nGaajB8m5Q0&t=32s), [02:00–02:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nGaajB8m5Q0&t=120s), [03:27–03:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nGaajB8m5Q0&t=207s) | External parameters and spreadsheet organization support adjustment. Formulas, relative changes, and a short conversion/reload loop make trial and error practical. | Author combat values in validated tables with explicit units and a fresh-run reload action. Preserve the configuration of reproducible tests. |
| [Risk and Reward — 00:32–02:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FXqEykD5Ub4&t=32s), [02:50–04:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FXqEykD5Ub4&t=170s), [09:02–09:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FXqEykD5Ub4&t=542s) | Movement and positioning change exposure and opportunity; intercepting an attack lane can matter more than chasing. Risk/reward is one aspect of enjoyment and can conflict with accessibility. | Connect a readable attack commitment to a useful opening. Test whether the decision is understandable and accessible instead of simply increasing punishment. |
| [Squeeze and Release — 00:17–00:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TYh5SJb5gWk&t=17s), [01:10–02:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TYh5SJb5gWk&t=70s), [02:18–02:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TYh5SJb5gWk&t=138s) | Pressure followed by resolution and reward shapes satisfaction; progress can open choices. Understand the purpose of a technique instead of imitating its surface. | Alternate dangerous commitments with recovery and repositioning. Observe whether players feel relief and new opportunity after responding well. |
| [Breaking Down Attack Animations — 00:12–00:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LewXWM7HDd8&t=12s), [00:43–02:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LewXWM7HDd8&t=43s), [02:39–03:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LewXWM7HDd8&t=159s) | Anticipation, attack, and follow-through serve different roles. An immediate pose acknowledges input and signals danger. Animators need intended poses, hit timing, total duration, and cancel timing. | Specify charge, release, and recovery explicitly, including cancel rules. Drive presentation from those phases without allowing animation timing to change authoritative hits. |
| [Attack Poses — 00:29–01:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=99gdMDF7V2E&t=29s), [01:09–01:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=99gdMDF7V2E&t=69s), [01:43–02:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=99gdMDF7V2E&t=103s) | The impact pose leaves a strong impression. Exaggeration, asymmetry, twist, silhouette, and the actual camera affect readability. | Judge prototype attacks at gameplay scale and angle. Replace an unclear pose before spending effort on extra intermediate frames. |
| [Eight Hit Stop Techniques — 00:58–02:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tycbMSjDDLg&t=58s), [02:12–03:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tycbMSjDDLg&t=132s), [03:45–04:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tycbMSjDDLg&t=225s) | Impact offsets can leave hurtboxes stationary. Grounded shake, tapering, pose blending, power, camera distance, and multiple fighters influence how much feedback works. | Keep recoil and shake visual. Scale effects to readability, support reduced motion, and reassess feedback density as team size grows. |
| [Clarity vs. Style — 00:34–01:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UjW_TTNtXEM&t=34s), [02:21–03:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UjW_TTNtXEM&t=141s) | Clarity and style depend on the game. Size, grouping, luminance, and typography guide the eye while the presentation should fit the world and audience. | Keep urgent commands and attack cues easy to find in the actual battle layout. Evaluate visual style against the player's next decision. |

## Extending this library

Add a lesson only when it helps a concrete feature decision. Record the source title/link, inspected timestamp range, retrieval language/limitations, a short paraphrase, and a clearly separate possible application. Recheck interpretation when a design depends on a fine distinction in imperfect captions. Do not turn the eight examples into a mandatory checklist for unrelated features.
